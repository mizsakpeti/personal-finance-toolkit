The Two Levers of Wealth: Time and Consistency

You don't need a large sum to start investing. You need a regular habit and enough time to let it compound.

TL;DR
  • Regular monthly contributions compound dramatically over time — even modest amounts.
  • Starting early matters more than starting big. Delaying by 5 years typically costs around 30–40% of your final portfolio.
  • Fees quietly eat into returns: a 1.5% annual cost vs. 0.2% index ETF often makes a six-figure difference over 20 years.
↓ Jump to the calculator

The most common reason people give for not investing is "I don't have enough to start." It's a reasonable-sounding excuse, and it's almost always wrong. The math of compound growth doesn't require a large starting amount. It requires two things: regular contributions, and time.

The problem is that modest amounts feel invisible at first. Putting away 30,000 forints a month doesn't feel like building wealth — it feels like a small transfer that won't amount to anything significant. The issue is that our intuitions about growth are linear, and compound growth is not. After one year, the change is barely visible. After ten, it's unmistakable. After twenty, it's the kind of number that makes people wish they had started sooner.

"The best time to plant a tree was twenty years ago. The second best time is now."

This calculator models the building phase: what happens when you contribute regularly, choose a portfolio allocation, and let compound interest do its work over time. Two numbers will probably surprise you — how much of the final value came from growth rather than your own contributions, and how much starting a few years later would have cost.

A note on allocation: the split between bonds and stocks changes the shape of your journey as much as the destination. Bonds provide stability; stocks provide long-run growth. Neither extreme is right for everyone. The allocation slider lets you find your own balance — and see immediately how it affects outcomes.

How to read this

The gap between the portfolio line and the contributions line is compound growth — money your money earned for you. In the early years it's barely visible. Around the halfway mark it starts to accelerate. By year 20 or 30, it typically dwarfs what you actually deposited. This is why starting early, even with small amounts, matters more than most people expect.

The dashed line is the cost-free ideal: what your portfolio would be with zero fees. The gap between the solid and dashed lines is what annual expense ratios quietly remove over time. A 1.5% annual cost sounds trivial — it's the difference between an index ETF and an actively managed fund sold at a bank. At 20-year horizons on a modest portfolio, that gap typically runs into several million forints.

The compounding surprise On a 20-year horizon with 30,000 Ft/month contributions and a 7% blended return, roughly 55–60% of the final portfolio value came from compound growth — not from the money you deposited. Most people who see this number for the first time find it difficult to believe.

What to do next

The two practical questions this calculator raises: what should you invest in, and where should you hold it?

  • For bonds (HU): PMÁP and FIXMÁP are available directly at allampapir.hu. Buy directly, not through a bank that charges custody fees.
  • For stocks / index funds: A low-cost MSCI World index ETF through Interactive Brokers or Lightyear is a common choice for Hungarian retail investors. Both are accessible in English and Hungarian, with no account minimums.
  • Account type: Consider opening a TBSZ account (Tartós Befektetési Számla). After a 5-year lock-in, gains are fully tax-free — a meaningful advantage for long-horizon portfolios.

Further reading: Kiszámoló covers Hungarian personal finance in accessible, jargon-free Hungarian. It's probably the best starting point for anyone new to this.

How the calculation works

The portfolio is compounded monthly. Each month, the current balance earns one twelfth of the annual blended return, then the monthly contribution is added. The annual contribution growth is applied once per year at year-end. The blended return is the weighted average of bond and stock yields, net of the annual expense ratio.

$$r_{\text{blended}} = w_{\text{bond}} \cdot r_{\text{bond}} + w_{\text{stock}} \cdot r_{\text{stock}}$$ $$r_{\text{net}} = r_{\text{blended}} - r_{\text{cost}}$$ $$P_{m+1} = P_m \cdot \left(1 + \frac{r_{\text{net}}}{12}\right) + C_{\text{month}}$$ $$C_{\text{year}+1} = C_{\text{year}} \cdot (1 + g)$$ $$\text{Real value} = \frac{FV}{(1 + r_{\text{inflation}})^n}$$

Where w are the portfolio weights (summing to 1), C is the monthly contribution, g is the annual contribution growth rate, and n is the number of years. The cost-free ideal uses rblended in place of rnet.
