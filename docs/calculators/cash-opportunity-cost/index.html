The Hidden Cost of Saving

Every time you hold money to spend later, there's a cost. Most people never see it — here's a calculator to make it concrete.

TL;DR
  • Saving costs money. Every day you hold cash, inflation silently erodes its value — and you miss what it could earn.
  • A standard bank account is often a real-terms loss, not safety.
  • Hungarian state bonds (FIXMÁP, MÁP+) carry the same low risk as a bank deposit, but return several times more.
↓ Jump to the calculator

When I joined MSCI as a developer, one of the first conversations in onboarding stayed with me. A senior colleague was walking us through what MSCI actually does — equity indices, risk models, portfolio analytics — and somewhere in there he paused and said:

"I genuinely hope nobody here has more than €1,500 sitting in a bank account that isn't earning a return. Because you all understand better than most what that's costing you."

And another remark from that same conversation has stuck with me just as much:

"You are paying a silent cost of saving up for later."

Think about it. You are almost never at true financial zero — you either have savings, or debt, or both. And since your salary arrives as money rather than goods, there is always a window between receiving it and spending it. That window is saving: choosing to spend later. On any given day, the cost of that deferral is small — almost imperceptible. But if you're fortunate enough to consistently spend less than you earn, that idle balance grows month after month. And as it grows, so does the cost of keeping it in the wrong place. A few percentage points difference on a small sum barely registers. On years of accumulated savings, it compounds into a completely different story.

This is especially true in Hungary. Talking openly about personal finance is still uncommon — money conversations rarely happen around the family table, and there's no cultural tradition of passing this kind of knowledge down. The result isn't just a gap in awareness; it's an accumulation of misinformation. "Investing is risky." "That's for wealthy people." "The stock market is gambling." These ideas are widespread, and for the low-risk instruments this article is about, they're mostly wrong.

Previous generations navigated a completely different economic reality, without the tools, products, or cultural framework that would have made these decisions obvious. What has changed is the entry barrier. Smartphone apps and online platforms have made it genuinely easy to open a bond account, track your savings, and find plain-language explanations written for ordinary people — not finance professionals. The tools are a few taps away. What still matters most is the conversation: one honest exchange with someone who has already thought about these questions can cut through more confusion than months of passive reading.

How to read this

The gap between the two lines is money that exists in one scenario but not the other. It doesn't require taking on more risk — Hungarian government bonds are direct obligations of the state. The difference is almost entirely explained by the fact that bonds require a tiny bit more effort to buy.

Toggle on "today's purchasing power" and the picture gets starker. The cash line may actually curve downward over time — meaning your money buys less in the future than it does today, even with the deposit interest. This is the inflation effect made visible. A positive nominal return can be a negative real return.

The key insight A 1% deposit rate with 4.5% inflation is not a 1% gain. It's a −3.3% real loss per year (Fisher equation). After 20 years at this rate, your purchasing power is roughly halved — even though the number in your account is larger.

What to do with this

Understanding the scale of the loss is step one. Acting on it takes an afternoon at most. Two good places to start:

  • Kiszámoló — a well-known Hungarian personal finance blog that explains money decisions in plain language, without jargon.
  • Magyar Állampapír kalkulátor — shows exactly what state bonds would earn on your specific amount, updated for current rates.

This isn't about becoming an investor who watches stock prices. It's a small, one-time decision — moving idle savings to a better instrument — that takes an afternoon and pays off every year after. The effort is minimal; the long-term difference is not.

  • Hungary: FIXMÁP and MÁP+ are available at allampapir.hu. Buy directly — not through a bank, which adds fees.
  • EU: Term deposits and short-term government bonds are accessible through most online brokerage accounts at noticeably better rates than a standard savings account.

A note on risk: this calculator focuses on the safest, most liquid part of your savings — the emergency buffer most people carry, typically around 3–6 months of expenses. That money should stay accessible and not be exposed to market volatility. There are riskier instruments with higher expected returns worth exploring for longer-term savings — but that's a separate conversation. The point here is narrower: even your most conservative savings has a better home. And for many people, more money sits in that "safe" pile than actually needs to.

How the calculation works

Compound interest applied annually. The "real value" conversion uses the exact Fisher equation rather than the simplified approximation — this makes a meaningful difference at timeframes beyond 10 years.

$$FV_{\text{cash}} = P \cdot (1 + r_{\text{deposit}})^n$$ $$FV_{\text{invested}} = P \cdot (1 + r_{\text{bond}})^n$$ $$\text{Opportunity cost} = FV_{\text{invested}} - FV_{\text{cash}}$$ $$\text{Real value} = \dfrac{FV}{(1 + r_{\text{inflation}})^n}$$ $$r_{\text{real}} = \dfrac{1 + r_{\text{nominal}}}{1 + r_{\text{inflation}}} - 1 \quad \text{(Fisher equation)}$$ $$\text{Real opportunity cost} = \dfrac{FV_{\text{invested}} - FV_{\text{cash}}}{(1 + r_{\text{inflation}})^n}$$
