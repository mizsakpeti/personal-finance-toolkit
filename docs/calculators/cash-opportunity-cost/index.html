The Cost of Playing It Too Safe

What sitting on cash is really costing you — and a calculator to make it concrete.

When I joined MSCI as a developer, one of the first conversations in onboarding stayed with me long after. A senior colleague was walking us through what MSCI actually does — equity indices, risk models, portfolio analytics — and somewhere in there he paused and said something like:

"I genuinely hope nobody here has more than €1,500 sitting in a bank account that isn't earning a return. Because you all understand better than most what that's costing you."

It landed. He wasn't being judgmental — he was making a point about financial literacy. People who work in finance live and breathe the concept that idle capital has a cost. Everyone else mostly doesn't think about it, or dismisses investing as "not their thing" or "too risky."

This is especially common in Hungary. Talking openly about money, savings, and investments is still somewhat taboo in many families. The default mental model is: save up, put it in the bank, don't take risks. That caution is understandable — it comes from lived experience. But there's a quiet cost to never questioning it, and most people never see it made explicit.

Here's the thing: keeping money in a savings account feels safe. And it is safe from loss — but it's not safe from erosion. Inflation eats your purchasing power year after year whether you notice or not. Meanwhile, Hungarian government bonds like PMÁP or Bónusz MÁP are also considered low-risk instruments, yet they consistently return several percentage points more than a standard deposit rate. The gap between "money in an account" and "money in a bond" isn't really about risk tolerance. It's mostly about inertia.

The calculator below makes that gap visible. A few percentage points of difference, compounded over ten or twenty years, becomes a very concrete number. The chart is the point — the diverging lines are a picture of a decision most people make passively, without ever realising it.

How to read this

The gap between the two lines is money that exists in one scenario but not the other. It doesn't require taking on more risk — government bonds in Hungary are backed by the state, just like deposits up to the guarantee limit. The difference is almost entirely explained by the fact that bonds require a tiny bit more effort to buy.

Toggle on "today's purchasing power" and the picture gets starker. The cash line may actually curve downward over time — meaning your money buys less in the future than it does today, even with the deposit interest. This is the inflation effect made visible. A positive nominal return can be a negative real return.

The key insight A 1% deposit rate with 4.5% inflation is not a 1% gain. It's a −3.3% real loss per year (Fisher equation). After 20 years, your purchasing power has shrunk significantly — even though the number in your account is larger.

What to do with this

This calculator isn't arguing for risk. It's arguing for awareness. A few practical steps that require very little financial sophistication:

  • Hungary: PMÁP and Bónusz MÁP are available directly through ÁKK (the Hungarian Government Debt Management Agency) or most major banks. They're denominated in HUF, state-backed, and in recent years have consistently beaten deposit rates.
  • EU: Most banks now offer term deposit products with noticeably better rates than standard savings accounts. EU government bonds are accessible through most brokerage accounts.
  • General: Even moving money from a 0% current account to a 2–3% savings account eliminates a large part of the passive loss shown here. The first step doesn't have to be complicated.

The point isn't to become an investor overnight. It's just to stop making a costly passive decision by default.

How the calculation works

Compound interest applied annually. The "real value" conversion uses the exact Fisher equation rather than the simplified approximation — this makes a meaningful difference at timeframes beyond 10 years.

$$FV_{\text{cash}} = P \cdot (1 + r_{\text{deposit}})^n$$ $$FV_{\text{invested}} = P \cdot (1 + r_{\text{bond}})^n$$ $$\text{Opportunity cost} = FV_{\text{invested}} - FV_{\text{cash}}$$ $$\text{Real value} = \dfrac{FV}{(1 + r_{\text{inflation}})^n}$$ $$r_{\text{real}} = \dfrac{1 + r_{\text{nominal}}}{1 + r_{\text{inflation}}} - 1 \quad \text{(Fisher equation)}$$ $$\text{Real opportunity cost} = \dfrac{FV_{\text{invested}} - FV_{\text{cash}}}{(1 + r_{\text{inflation}})^n}$$

Preset rates are approximate figures based on publicly available data for early 2025: Hungarian PMÁP / Bónusz MÁP rates via ÁKK, KSH inflation forecasts, ECB deposit facility rates, and average EU government bond yields. Always verify current rates before making any financial decisions. This is not financial advice.
